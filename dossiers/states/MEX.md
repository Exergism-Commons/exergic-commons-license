# Mexico

> **Governance record only.** This dossier does not itself create licensing restrictions. Only a versioned Schedule expressly incorporated by a software release has operative effect.

## 1. Current determination

**S — Restricted with defined scope.** The finding follows attributable security/disappearance/torture projects and concrete coercive data deployments. It does not treat the Mexican federation, biometric identity infrastructure or organized-crime violence as Restricted by default.

## 2. ECL criteria engaged

- enforced disappearance, torture and coercive targeting/unlawful violence;
- obstruction of search, investigation and meaningful remedy;
- repressive surveillance or irreversible coercive capture where a concrete biometric/data deployment actually produces those effects.

## 3. Evidence supporting the determination

On 17 April 2026 the UN Committee on Enforced Disappearances decided, under article 34 of the Convention, to bring Mexico's situation to the attention of the UN General Assembly after reviewing information concerning widespread/systematic enforced disappearance, persistent impunity, alleged involvement or acquiescence of public officials and serious deficiencies in search and investigation. The Committee's record also documents the coexistence of direct State involvement, alleged official-criminal collusion and large volumes of disappearances attributable to non-State actors.

Mexican human-rights bodies continue issuing grave-violation recommendations attributing enforced disappearance and torture to identified security or military personnel. CNDH Recommendation 206VG/2026, for example, attributes forced disappearance, torture and deprivation of life to Navy personnel in Guasave, Sinaloa. Separately, Mexico is expanding national biometric identity/data infrastructure; that fact creates a reviewable capability but does not itself prove an ECL-prohibited use.

## 4. Counter-evidence and exergic institutions

Mexico maintains federal and state search commissions, specialized prosecutors, the CNDH, courts and victim/search institutions that investigate, document and sometimes attribute official abuse. Mexico has also cooperated with the Committee's procedures. These institutions, plus federal fragmentation and the substantial independent role of organized-crime actors, weigh decisively against a whole-State finding.

## 5. Attribution and exclusions

Scope follows **identified federal, state or municipal security and military-public-security units/projects materially involved in enforced disappearance, torture, unlawful coercive detention/targeting, official collusion/acquiescence or material obstruction of search/accountability**. Biometric identity/data systems enter scope **only when a concrete deployment materially enables coercive surveillance, discriminatory targeting or irreversible institutional capture**. Search commissions, CNDH/remedial functions, independent courts, victims' organizations, ordinary identity administration and non-attributable private criminal conduct are excluded.

## 6. Adversarial determination

**UPHOLD / NARROW S.** Current disappearance evidence strongly preserves a scoped restriction, but the first-pass formulation was too broad insofar as it could imply that military/public-security or biometric systems were Restricted as classes without project-specific attribution.

## 7. Review / removal triggers

Review on measurable reduction and effective prosecution of attributable enforced disappearance, protection/resourcing of search functions, remedies for torture, accountable public-security practice and binding safeguards around coercive biometric/data use. Narrow or remove individual scopes when the relevant project is terminated or durably remediated.
