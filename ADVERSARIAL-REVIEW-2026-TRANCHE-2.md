### #1 Afghanistan — **UPHOLD R**

**Counter-evidence credited:** some individual detainees are released after short periods, humanitarian/development cooperation with international organizations persists, and earlier Taliban decrees formally recognized limited rights such as consent to marriage and inheritance.

**Evidence against downgrade:** UNAMA reported in May 2026 that Decree No. 18 further entrenches systemic discrimination against women and girls in law and practice. ### #11 Azerbaijan — **UPHOLD R**

**Counter-evidence credited:** Azerbaijan has resumed cooperation with the Council of Europe's CPT, retains formal courts and legal institutions, and is subject to judgments of the European Court of Human Rights.

**Evidence against downgrade:** PACE Resolution 2663 (24 June 2026) describes a systemic pattern of silencing critical voices, reports 328 political prisoners as of May 2026, highlights the politically motivated renewed prosecution of Anar Mammadli, and notes a high number of leading ECtHR judgments still awaiting implementation. The coercive pattern spans criminal prosecution, detention, media law, party law and NGO regulation.

**Finding:** formal legal institutions and renewed CPT cooperation are meaningful but have not demonstrated effective restraint of the cross-institutional repression. `R` survives.

Evidence/counter-evidence:
- https://pace.coe.int/en/files/36229/html
- https://pace.coe.int/en/files/35803/html

### #16 Belarus — **UPHOLD R**

**Counter-evidence credited:** negotiated releases/expulsions of some prisoners have occurred and the government has responded to some UN communications.

**Evidence against downgrade:** 2026 UN Special Procedures communications continue to document politically motivated detention, prolonged incommunicado/solitary confinement, ill-treatment, inadequate medical care and due-process violations. The Human Rights Council adopted a new Belarus resolution at its 61st session in March 2026.

**Finding:** isolated releases do not establish institutional remediation of the detention, security, judicial and information-control system. `R` survives.

### #27 Burkina Faso — **UPHOLD R**

**Counter-evidence credited:** a formal Justice Ministry continues to state rule-of-law, human-rights and judicial-independence functions, so legal institutions cannot be treated as nonexistent.

**Evidence against downgrade:** the military governing structure continues to centralize political control; a decree effective February 2026 requires NGOs and associations to domicile all liquid accounts with a public Treasury institution, prompting UN Special Procedures concern over association, expression, assembly and humanitarian access. The governing authorities have extended military transition and political repression rather than restoring a genuinely independent civilian accountability structure.

**Finding:** the current counter-institutions do not show material independence or effective capacity to restrain the military governing apparatus. `R` survives.

### #28 Burundi — **UPHOLD R, REVIEW OMBUDSMAN EFFECTIVENESS**

**Counter-evidence credited:** Burundi's Ombudsman presented a 2025 activity report in January 2026 and described institutional reforms aimed at rights protection, rule of law and good governance.

**Evidence against downgrade:** the UN Committee against Torture published multiple January 2026 decisions concerning torture/ill-treatment, lack of effective investigation/redress and, in one case, reprisals for cooperation with the Committee. Several proceedings record failure by the State party to cooperate. The evidence directly tests whether domestic remedy structures are effective rather than merely formal.

**Finding:** the Ombudsman is relevant counter-evidence but has not yet demonstrated remediation strong enough to rebut the cross-institutional repression basis. `R` survives provisionally. A later review should examine whether Ombudsman reforms produce releases, investigations or binding remedies in political/torture cases.

### #42 Cuba — **UPHOLD R, CREDIT 2026 CLEMENCY**

**Counter-evidence credited:** Cuba announced the release of 51 prisoners in March 2026 and a pardon covering 2,010 convicted persons in April 2026. These are material acts of clemency and must be recorded rather than ignored.

**Evidence against downgrade:** a January 2026 UN Special Procedures communication describes an alleged systematic pattern of criminalizing dissent and using coercion including arbitrary detention and forced exile against defenders, activists and artists. The broad clemency measures do not establish that the political surveillance/criminalization system has ceased or that independent review mechanisms now constrain it.

**Finding:** the releases reduce individual harm but do not currently rebut the cross-institutional ECL basis. `R` survives, with future review focused on whether clemency becomes durable legal/institutional reform.

### #51 Egypt — **UPHOLD R; MANDATORY REVIEW AFTER 1 OCTOBER 2026**

**Counter-evidence credited:** Egypt enacted a new Criminal Procedure Law with stated safeguards on pretrial detention, judicial review, counsel and privacy. It is scheduled to enter into force on **1 October 2026**.

**Evidence against downgrade at current cutoff:** the reform is not yet operative. In April 2026 UN Special Procedures raised allegations from North Sinai including unlawful killings, mass arbitrary detention, enforced disappearance, secret detention, torture, mass displacement and destruction of civilian property, implicating Egyptian security forces and government-supported militias and potentially amounting to war crimes/crimes against humanity. Other recent communications continue to describe use of national-security/counterterrorism law against peaceful activity.

**Finding:** a future reform cannot presently rebut an ongoing cross-institutional record. `R` survives at the 11 August cutoff. ECL governance should require a fresh review after the law has operated long enough to test actual implementation.

### #54 Eritrea — **UPHOLD R**

**Counter-evidence credited:** the 2026 Human Rights Council debate noted some encouraging developments, including an OHCHR human-rights training in Asmara and release of some arbitrarily detained persons; the EU also noted increased dialogue with Eritrea.

**Evidence against downgrade:** in July 2026 the Human Rights Council renewed the Special Rapporteur mandate for another year and continued to call on Eritrea to cooperate fully with OHCHR and the mandate. The current record continues to concern arbitrary detention, enforced disappearance and prolonged/indefinite national service; Eritrea has not established independent remedies capable of constraining these core systems.

**Finding:** releases/training are meaningful but insufficient to demonstrate systemic cessation or accountability. `R` survives.

### #77 India — **DOWNGRADE R → S**

**Original:** whole State apparatus Restricted.

**Counter-evidence / institutional attribution:** India has a federal constitutional structure and an independent Supreme Court with direct Article 32 jurisdiction to enforce Fundamental Rights through habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto and certiorari. The Court expressly has power to invalidate legislation or governmental action that violates the Constitution and continues to issue active judgments and public-law orders in 2026. Treating the entire Union-and-State governmental apparatus as a single unreviewable coercive actor therefore overstates attribution.

**Evidence preserving a scoped restriction:** UN Special Procedures in March–April 2026 continued to raise serious concerns about prolonged UAPA detention without trial, the compatibility of UAPA with international human-rights obligations, the Armed Forces Special Powers Act, and other security-law practices. These are concrete ECL issues but attach more accurately to specified Union/state security, counterterrorism, detention, digital-censorship, punitive-demolition and discriminatory-enforcement projects.

**Finding:** the original `R` is overbroad. **DOWNGRADE to `S — Restricted with defined scope`.** The future Schedule should identify the responsible security/legal/administrative projects rather than restricting unrelated courts, health systems, scientific agencies, municipalities or other public functions merely because they form part of India.
